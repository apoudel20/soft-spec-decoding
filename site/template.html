Soft speculative decoding

Target-guided draft trees for lossless speculative decoding

A small model drafts tokens and a large model checks them, so the output stays exactly the large model's. This project asks whether the large model's internal view of its upcoming text can decide which drafts are worth checking. Most results so far reproduce known work. The selection question in section 4 is open.

Code Results Literature review
01

The idea

Speculative decoding checks a tree of drafted tokens in one forward pass of the target. In current methods the drafter's own confidence decides how that tree grows. Here a summary read from the target's middle layers decides it instead.

02

Two trees

The drafter can afford hundreds of candidates. The target can check only a few dozen per pass, at about the cost of one decode step. We found no published method that uses information from the target to choose which nodes go into its KV-cache tree (Figure 3, top). Branches the target doesn't take can't be reused later: under exact verification the next round always starts outside the checked tree.

    Better selection is worth most where the drafter is weakest and the budget is small. At 32 nodes the ceiling is +36% on chat, +20% on code and +13% on math. That clears our go line of 20% on chat and code. These are upper bounds, and a real selector would recover only part of each gap.

    Two caveats. On chat the forest often misses the target’s path entirely, so even perfect selection reaches 5.2 of 8: how the forest is grown matters there too. And on this 4-bit setup, checking 128 nodes cost the same as checking one (about 100 ms), because the 4-bit kernels dominate. Real inference kernels have a knee, usually in the tens of nodes, and that knee decides which column above matters.

    Done · layer sweep · Qwen3-8B (4-bit) and Qwen3-0.6B · 600 training answers, 40 held-out dev answers per set · every second layer

    A first pass suggested the 8B knew no more than the 0.6B about upcoming content, but that probe was underpowered: a 4,096-wide state fit on about 14,000 rows, which went below zero on chat. We reran it fairly, with 2.7 times the training rows, a wider regularisation range, and both models reduced to the same width (1,024) by PCA. The 8B is then ahead on every set:

    The edge on content is small and consistent (+0.02 to +0.03 R²). The edge on length and structure is larger (up to +0.08 on code) and sits at layer 18 of 36. Content information peaks later, at layers 24 to 36. So the target does know more about its own future, and the layers to tap differ by what is being predicted: middle layers for how long and what shape, upper layers for what it will say.

    Output matched the target’s own greedy text on 8 of 30 prompts. Any speculative method shows this on this setup. A strict exactness check needs the reference computed with the same chunked pass, or logits in fp32.

    Even a summary of the target’s true states for the very block being drafted does not help DFlash, whether the drafter is frozen or fine-tuned alongside it. Against the matched fine-tune, the difference is +0.04, −0.06 and −0.02 tokens, with 95% intervals that straddle zero. Fed raw and untrained, it hurts, because DFlash never saw such an input.

    With 300 training answers and a single averaged summary, knowing the future does not make this drafter better. Much more training might still find a use for it, but three ways of feeding a plan in have now been flat, oracle included. The remaining route is choosing better which drafted tokens get checked, which does not need the drafter to absorb a plan.

    Next · measuring tree headroom on DFlash

    A drafter built around a plan would need the plan to carry usable information, and the tests above say a strong block drafter does not use it. What is left is the tree: DFlash gives a distribution for every slot, so many alternative continuations can be checked in one pass. A tree version of DFlash already exists (DDTree); choosing which branches to check using the target’s own signal does not. The next measurement asks how much a tree on DFlash adds over its single chain, and how much room a perfect selector would still have.

    Bolting a plan onto DFlash does not help, so the next step is a drafter built around the plan: denoise a latent that stands for the next stretch of text (Sketch D), decode it coarse to fine (Sketch E), with each level reading a different tapped layer of the target (Sketch B) and several latent branches becoming a draft tree (Sketch C). It would compete with DFlash rather than ride on it. ChainFlow is the closest prior work, with one latent per token rather than one per stretch.

    Pending: a no-training test that feeds the target’s true future states through DFlash’s own context channel. Its result decides whether the new drafter reads the target through context attention or through the latent alone.

    ChainFlow

    Drafter predicts the target's next 8 compressed hidden states and picks 8,192 candidate tokens. Its DFlash baseline (2.35 accepted per pass on Qwen3.5-4B, thinking off) is far below DFlash’s card (5.9 to 7.7, thinking on), but our own DFlash run with thinking off gives a similar 2.4 on chat, so the gap may be the setup rather than a weak baseline.

    06

    Limitations

    • Most results use a 1.5B target, where any speculative method gains little.
    • 30 prompts per set. Differences under about 3% are noise.
    • Baselines are our own, not released EAGLE-3 or DFlash checkpoints.
    • The figures illustrate the design. They are not measurements.
