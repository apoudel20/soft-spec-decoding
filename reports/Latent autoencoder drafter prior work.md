# Prior work leaves only sampled-latent trees open

Every stage of the proposed drafter has close prior work, and most of the pipeline has already been built as one system. **ChainFlow (arXiv 2611.08669, Nov 2026) trains a VAE over a frozen Qwen3.5 target's final hidden states, predicts 8 future latents with a one-step rectified flow, decodes them through the frozen LM head and verifies a 32-node tree**, beating DFlash by 11% and EAGLE-3 by 39% in vLLM ([ChainFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08669)). FlashMap already merges several independent flow samples into a lossless tree on Qwen3-8B ([FlashMap](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24365)). Multi-Future Drafting (MFD) already builds a lossless mixture tree from K=8 learned discrete futures on Qwen3-8B, reaching **τ 9.47 and 6.51x against τ 8.60 for the best product tree** ([MFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13017)). What nobody has published is the intersection, a draft tree formed from several sampled continuous latents of the target's hidden states and verified exactly at T>0. The multi-layer grid and the token-KL loss are incremental changes. The prize is small. Coherent mixture trees beat identically trained product trees by about 5% to 8% in MFD, and by our arithmetic the autoencoder alone costs about 4% of chat acceptance and 6% of math acceptance for every point of top-1 disagreement it introduces. The stage-2 entry gate therefore has to be strict. We propose 99% teacher-forced top-1 agreement on chat and 99.5% on math and code, with mean total variation (TV) at or below 0.01 and 0.005. These numbers hold only after massive-activation dimensions are pinned to constants and the final row is scored in RMSNorm space. The cheapest decisive test trains one small predictor with three swappable heads (mean, sampled, discrete modes) on cached Qwen3-8B states and compares their trees offline at matched node budgets. If sampled latents do not beat the mean-latent product tree by at least 5% τ on chat, the open piece is closed.

Throughout, τ is mean accepted tokens per target forward pass and "x" is wall-clock speedup over autoregressive decoding. Our own results carry no citation. Thresholds marked as proposals come from our arithmetic, not from the literature.

## ChainFlow, FlashMap and MFD already cover each stage

The table maps each piece of the design to its nearest published work. It shows that what is left is a specific pairing of pieces, not any single component.

| Piece of the proposal | Closest prior work | What it already does | What is left |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-layer target states as conditioning | EAGLE-3, DFlash, FlashMap | EAGLE-3 fuses low, mid and high layers. DFlash injects 5 fused layers into every drafter K/V. FlashMap conditions on Qwen3-8B layers 1, 9, 17, 25, 33 | Nothing, as conditioning |
| Autoencoder over future hidden states | ChainFlow | Transformer VAE over windows of up to 16 final-layer states, Z=1024 per position for a 4096-wide target, KL β 3e-5 | Multi-layer rows, compression along time into one chunk latent |
| Chunk VAE recipe | CALM | 4 tokens into 128 dims at over 99.9% token accuracy, KL floor, latent dropout, sample-able energy head | Token inputs only, no hidden states, no drafting |
| RAE-style frozen encoder, trained decoder | RAE, LDLM | RAE works for vision. LDLM finds frozen GPT-2 states a poor diffusion space | Untested for drafting, and the text evidence is negative |
| Final row through frozen LM head with token loss | ChainFlow, EAGLE-1, DFlash | CE through the frozen lm_head plus a hidden-state error | KL instead of CE, a trivial change |
| Deterministic predictor of future latent | EAGLE-1, Lyanna, NextLat, ChainFlow | Next-state or multi-state prediction decoded by the head | Nothing |
| Diffusion or flow predictor | ChainFlow, FlashMap, CALM | One or two Euler steps suffice when conditioned on the target | Nothing |
| Several samples merged into a coherent tree | FlashMap, MFD, DBLast | Noise-draw trees over one-hot blocks, discrete-mode mixture trees, categorical branch latents | Continuous samples of a hidden-state latent |
| Exact verification at T>0 | MFD, DBLast, FlashMap | Lossless mixture trees and chains | ChainFlow is greedy only and not bit-exact |

ChainFlow is the paper a reviewer will cite first. Its drafters (529M to 1.64B parameters) predict K=8 future latents from 8 context latents with two chunk experts, then go through the target's frozen lm_head. It adds a path head over the last 8 parent tokens and a rank-256 Markov bias, because position-wise decoding of future hidden states loses inter-token dependence. Its loss mixes flow (0.2), hidden-state error (0.25), CE through the head (0.3), a coverage hinge, an acceptance surrogate and VAE reconstruction (0.5) ([ChainFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08669)). Its tree comes from beam search over token candidates from the posterior-mean latent, not from several latent samples, and the main text read does not report sampling. The limits are concrete. Verification is greedy only, **4% to 13% of prompts diverge from plain greedy decoding**, which the authors attribute to fp16 near-ties, and the chain-only drafter reaches τ 2.01 against 2.35 for DFlash in the same table ([ChainFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08669)). That ratio of about 0.86 is the best available prior for how a deterministic latent predictor compares with a token-level block drafter before branching is added. ChainFlow also compresses channels per position. The proposal's single latent per chunk of k positions follows CALM instead, which compressed 4 tokens into 128 dims but lost quality at K=8 ([CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27688v1)).

FlashMap and MFD own the branching half. FlashMap runs rectified flow from Gaussian noise to the one-hot encoding of a 16-token block, conditioned on five Qwen3-8B layers with the head frozen. It reports 6.14x on GSM8K against 5.77x for DFlash retrained on the same data, and **four independent noise draws merged into a 16-node tree add 4% to 24% speedup, about 11% on average**, over its own single sample ([FlashMap](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24365)). That gain is measured against a chain, not against a DDTree-style product tree, so it overstates what sampling adds over our baseline. MFD supplies the right comparison. It proves that a product tree holding two futures differing in m positions must also hold 2^(m-1)-1 chimera prefixes, measures **27% to 33% chimera nodes in 64-node product trees on Qwen3-4B and 8B**, and beats the identically trained single-mode tree by **4.8% to 7.4% at T=0 and 5.7% to 7.6% at T=1** ([MFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13017)). Its ablations bind the design. The mode must enter at the drafter input (+5.0 τ at B=64) rather than the last layer (+1.5) or a low-rank output map (-0.6). Averaging the loss over modes collapses to one mode, so winner-take-all training is required. The mode gate picks the best mode only 38% of the time at K=4, so the value comes from putting several modes in one tree, not from choosing one ([MFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13017)). DBLast adds a categorical latent to a DFlash-style drafter on Qwen3-8B and gains **+5.6% accepted length at high determinism and +12.1% at low determinism**, with most of the gain reached by K=4 ([DBLast](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.05448)). DDTree, the product-tree baseline, lifts Qwen3-8B MATH-500 τ from 7.79 to 10.73 and MT-Bench from 4.28 to 6.58 ([DDTree](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12989)).

The latent text diffusion literature supports feasibility but not the specific design. PLANNER, LD4LG, Large Concept Models, TextLDM and the 2026 flow LMs are standalone generators over latents from a separately trained text autoencoder or a sentence encoder, never over a frozen decoder-only LLM's states, and none drafts ([PLANNER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02531v3), [LD4LG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09462), [LCM](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.08821), [TextLDM](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07748v1)). Their useful lesson is that one-step continuous generation works. Flow map LMs beat 8-step discrete diffusion with a single step ([FMLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16813)). LCM's warning also transfers. Its MSE-regression variant regressed to averages and its SONAR space produced predictions that fell off the decodable manifold ([LCM](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.08821)). EAGLE-3 is the other caution. It removed EAGLE-1's feature-regression loss because it capped gains from more data, and step-1 features then stopped approximating true features ([EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840)). That argument targets a per-step regression anchor, not a decoded latent, but it says the predictor should be trained through the frozen head with token loss and treat latent regression as auxiliary. KVShot adds that on Qwen3-8B the top hidden state alone is a query-shaped summary that drops what later draft steps need ([KVShot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26412)), which supports conditioning the predictor on several layers.

## Raw Qwen3-8B states make a poor latent space

The geometry results argue against the pure RAE option and fix most of the normalization choices. RAE pairs a frozen DINOv2 encoder with a trained decoder and needs three fixes before diffusion works. Those are a model width at least the token dimension, a dimension-dependent noise shift α = sqrt(m/4096), and decoder noise augmentation ([RAE](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11690v1)). Applied literally to a 4 layer by 8 position grid of 4096-dim states, m is about 131k and α is about 5.7, and the width rule demands a predictor roughly as wide as the target. The text evidence is also negative. LDLM found frozen GPT-2 hidden states gave **Gen-PPL 149.7 and MAUVE 36.6, against 66.3 and 89.1 for a jointly trained latent** ([LDLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07933v1)). DiHAL ran diffusion bridges directly on Qwen3-8B. Raw hidden-space DDPM was the worst bridge (teacher KL 11.2), and a latent model with **hidden MSE 0.0067 still had teacher KL 5.74** in a reduced Llama setting ([DiHAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14368v2)). Capacity is not the problem. Cramming decodes 1568 tokens from one optimized input vector of Llama-3.1-8B, and ICAE reconstructs 512 tokens from 128 slots at 99.1 to 99.8 BLEU ([Cramming](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13063), [ICAE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06945)). Smoothness and decodability of predicted points are the problem. The design should use a trained compressing VAE on top of the frozen states, with CALM's KL floor (λ=0.5 per dim, β=0.001) and 0.15 latent dropout, or RAE's decoder noise, so the decoder tolerates predictor error ([CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27688v1)). With a total latent of 1024 to 8192 dims, α falls between 0.5 and 1.4 and the width rule allows a cheap predictor.

Normalization has to handle massive activations first. Qwen3-8B's **global max activation is 17,664 against a median of about 3.1 at the peak token** ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15572)). In LLaMA2 these sit in a few fixed dimensions on the first token and delimiters, and **setting them to their mean leaves perplexity unchanged (5.47) while zeroing them sends it to infinity** ([Sun et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17762v2)). An unweighted MSE over layers 18, 24 and 30 would spend its capacity on a handful of coordinates. The recipe that follows from the notes has four parts. Measure the massive dimensions and carrier tokens at layers 18, 24, 30 and 36, copy them as constants, and drop them and position 0 from the loss and from all statistics. Z-score the remaining dimensions of the middle rows with means and standard deviations from about 1M tokens, since 1 to 3 rogue dimensions otherwise dominate distances ([Timkey and van Schijndel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04404)). Gao-style mean subtraction with unit norm plus a separately predicted log-norm is the alternative ([Gao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04093v1)). For the final row, score RMSNorm(h)·g, the vector the head actually reads, with a cosine term plus KL to the target's logits. Norm error there is nearly free, while direction error is what hurts. SAE reconstructions cost **2.2 to 4.5x more KL than random errors of the same norm**, and rescaling to the SAE's norm costs almost nothing ([Gurnee](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/rZPiuFxESMxCDHe4B/sae-reconstruction-errors-are-empir)). Finally, the hidden state at position t+i predicts token t+i+1, so the grid must include the current position's final row for the k decoded tokens to line up with the tokens being drafted.

The middle rows are conditioning, not decodable targets. The raw logit lens fails before about two-thirds depth in GPT-Neo-2.7B, and early top-1 guesses are overturned 60% to 80% of the time in 8B-class models ([Tuned lens](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.08112v6), [Gupta et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18871v1)). Reconstructing future rows at layers 18, 24 and 30 can help only by making the latent smoother, which TextLDM's REPA result suggests. Aligning to the third-to-last layer of Qwen3-1.7B left reconstruction unchanged but raised diffusion MAUVE from 2.5 to 20.4 ([TextLDM](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07748v1)). That makes "final row only" against "full grid" a required ablation, not a default.

## One point of top-1 loss costs 4% to 6% of acceptance

Thresholds should be set in output space, because top-1 flip rate tracks teacher-forced TV with **a median ratio of 1.05**, TV predicts greedy draft acceptance within 1.1% to 2.4%, and KL misranks compressors 11% of the time ([Felicio](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00694)). We sized the gate with a simple model. Our chains fit a geometric acceptance curve over a 16-token block with per-position acceptance p of about 0.75 on chat (τ 2.96), 0.90 on math (τ 7.46) and 0.89 on code (τ 6.77). If the autoencoder independently corrupts a fraction 1-r of positions, p becomes p·r. This treats AE and predictor errors as independent and ignores tree structure, so it is a rough budget, not a prediction.

| AE top-1 agreement r | Chat chain τ (base 2.96) | Math chain τ (base 7.46) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.995 | -1.9% | -3.1% |
| 0.99 | -3.7% | -6.1% |
| 0.98 | -7.3% | -11.8% |

Against an expected coherence gain of 5% to 8%, an autoencoder at 98% agreement erases the whole benefit on every domain, and 99% erases most of it on math and code. Published reconstruction makes the stricter bar plausible but not certain. CALM reaches over 99.9% at K=4 and TextLDM 97.6% to 100% depending on corpus, but both reconstruct tokens rather than future hidden states ([CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27688v1), [TextLDM](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07748v1)). ChainFlow did not report reconstruction accuracy in the pages read.

We read "stage 2" as the deterministic search network, so the gate has an entry half (the autoencoder) and an exit half (the predictor). The proposed **entry gate** has four conditions. Teacher-forced top-1 agreement of LM_head(decode(encode(grid))) with the target's own argmax must reach at least 99% on chat and 99.5% on math and code. Mean TV against the target's distribution must be at or below 0.01 on chat and 0.005 on math and code. The earlier proposal of TV 0.02 is too loose once flips track TV at a ratio near 1. Agreement may drop by at most 1 point when Gaussian noise at the scale of the predictor's expected residual is added to the latent. CALM's posterior standard deviation of about 0.3 is a reasonable first scale. Everything is reported by target-margin bucket, because flips concentrate at near-ties, and a 4-bit target has narrower top-1/top-2 margins than a 16-bit one ([Quantized margins](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.03051)). KL is reported but not gated. Mean KL should still sit well under 0.1 nats, since DiHAL's KL of 0.53 on Qwen3-8B meant a 10.6x perplexity blowup ([DiHAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14368v2)). Pick the smallest latent that passes. Start at k=8 with total latents of 512, 1024 and 2048 dims (64 to 256 per position), between CALM's 32 per token and ChainFlow's 1024, and try k=16 only after k=8 passes. A learned-error check belongs in the gate as well. If the AE's flips exceed those of random noise at the same norm by more than about 2x, as Gurnee found for SAEs, isotropic noise tests overstate tolerance and the noise condition must use learned errors.

The proposed **exit gate** for the deterministic predictor is relative to DFlash on the same data. Its decoded chain should reach at least 0.85 of the DFlash chain τ (about 2.5 on chat, 6.3 on math, 5.75 on code), using ChainFlow's 0.86 chain ratio as the prior. Its per-slot top-4 product tree should land within 10% of our DFlash top-4 trees (about 4.0, 9.4 and 9.6). A mean predictor is expected to regress toward averages, as LCM's MSE variant did, so missing the chain bar is not fatal by itself. Missing both bars means the latent loses more than sampling can recover and diffusion should not start.

## What remains is MFD with sampled continuous modes

Strip away the incremental parts and the open claim is narrow. A latent decoded through the frozen head gives, for each sample z, a product distribution over k positions, and several samples give a mixture of product distributions. That is exactly MFD's object, with MFD's K learned mode embeddings replaced by draws from a predictor over a hidden-state latent. Losslessness at T>0 is not a hard part of it. Conditional on the N drawn latents, the finite mixture q_N(x) = (1/N) Σ_j q(x | z_j) is a fixed proposal that is independent of the verification coins, so standard tree rejection sampling with q_N stays exact, as with MFD's discrete mixture. This is our derivation and needs checking before it goes in a paper. The intractable marginal over continuous z never has to be computed. The open question is empirical. Does sampling a continuous latent cover the target's futures better than 8 winner-take-all modes, given that DBLast's gains saturate by K=4 to 8 and MFD's trees remain 22% to 24% chimeras even with modes ([DBLast](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.05448), [MFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13017))? The second open piece is the representation itself. No paper predicts multi-layer future hidden states as the latent, and ChainFlow uses only the final layer.

Our own results sharpen the picture in two ways. First, our DFlash per-slot top-4 trees already reach **4.43 on chat against 2.96 for the chain (+50%), 10.49 against 7.46 on math (+41%) and 10.72 against 6.77 on code (+58%)**. Those are product trees, so by MFD's measurement roughly a quarter to a third of their nodes are chimeras, and they are the baseline a sampled-latent tree must beat. MFD's 5% to 8% margin over product trees suggests a ceiling near 4.7 on chat and 11.3 on math, before any autoencoder cost. Second, plan conditioning added as an extra DFlash input was flat even with the oracle future. MFD reports that oracle mode choice would make chains 20% to 40% longer. The two results reconcile if our plan representation did not identify the token block, whereas MFD's modes are trained winner-take-all against the target block. The lesson for this proposal is double-edged. Its reconstruction-through-the-head objective does force the latent to name the block, which our plan vector did not. But the null also says that adding more target information as conditioning buys nothing, so any gain must come from branch coherence in the tree, not from what the latent tells the drafter.

Two systems limits cap the payoff further. MFD falls behind plain DDTree from batch 8, and ChainFlow's tree helps only up to about 4 concurrent requests and drops to 0.94x at 8 ([MFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13017), [ChainFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08669)). Latent branching is a batch-1 technique. Decoding N samples through a 151,936-row head is also N times the head GEMMs per position. Lyanna's trick of batching all positions into one GEMM cut draft latency by 60.9% and applies here ([Lyanna](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21224v1)). The honest novelty is a workshop-to-conference delta. It would read as "continuous-latent MFD over target hidden states" and would need to beat MFD, FlashMap's multi-sample tree and DDTree in one harness on Qwen3-8B. The search was web-based with patchy 2026 indexing. ChainFlow's ablations, FlashMap's appendix and several MFD-cited papers (TreeFlash, D-PACE, UBTree) were not read in full, so one of them could already sample continuous latents.

## A one-week offline test settles the open piece

The cheapest experiment isolates sampling from representation by holding everything else fixed. Cache layers 18, 24, 30 and pre-norm 36 of 4-bit Qwen3-8B on target-regenerated responses for about 2,000 mixed chat, math and code prompts. At 32 KB per position in fp16, 300k to 500k positions take 10 to 16 GB of disk. The same pass gives the massive-activation scan, the normalization statistics and the analytic flip radius r = (z_i − z_j) / ‖(W_i − W_j) ⊙ g‖ for the margin distribution. Train the k=8 VAE at the three latent sizes and apply the entry gate. If no size passes, stop there, because the autoencoder alone eats the plausible gain.

If the gate passes, train one small predictor that reads the current multi-layer state and has three swappable output heads on identical data and steps. Head A regresses the mean latent. Head B is a CALM-style energy head with a noise input, the cheapest one-step stochastic sampler, standing in for diffusion as ChainFlow and FlashMap show one step suffices ([CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27688v1)). Head C uses K=8 learned mode embeddings at the input with winner-take-all training, an MFD replica. Decode every head through the frozen AE decoder and head. Build trees at matched node budgets of 16, 32 and 64. Arm A uses per-slot top-k of the mean latent, B merges N=4 and 8 samples into a mixture tree, and C uses MFD's mixture rule. Score them offline by teacher forcing against the target's greedy continuation, with no tree kernel needed, and report τ, chimera rate and accepted length by domain. Our DFlash top-4 trees are an external reference, not a matched comparison.

The decision rule is fixed in advance. **Proceed to a full diffusion predictor only if B beats A by at least 5% τ at matched budget on chat and is at least even with C.** Kill the sampled-latent idea if B beats A by under 2% or C beats B, since a discrete mode head then does the job more cheaply. If B wins but the gated autoencoder plus predictor falls below DFlash's product tree, drop the autoencoder and inject sampled noise at DFlash's input, which MFD's placement ablation favors. A T=1 run of arm B with q_N verification on 200 prompts, compared with the target's own sampled token distribution, closes the exactness question at the same time.

## Conclusion

The design's center of gravity has moved. The autoencoder, the multi-layer grid and the diffusion predictor once looked like the contribution. In light of ChainFlow and the geometry results they are costs, each of which can only lose acceptance relative to a drafter that predicts tokens directly. The only component that can add acceptance is the coherence of sampled branches, and MFD has already shown that coherence is worth single-digit percentages over product trees. The autoencoder is justified only if a continuous hidden-state latent is a better space to sample futures from than learned modes or input noise, and that is now a one-week offline question rather than an architecture to build.

The comparison also changes what our earlier null result means. The flat oracle-plan experiment looked like evidence against latent planning in general. Next to MFD it reads more narrowly. Conditioning that does not identify the token block is useless, while a latent trained to reconstruct the block through the head might not be. If arm B wins on chat, where our chain-to-tree gap and DBLast's low-determinism gains are both largest, the paper is about sampled futures and should drop the planning framing. If it loses, the result is a clean negative that closes the latent-drafter line on Qwen3-8B with numbers rather than argument.
